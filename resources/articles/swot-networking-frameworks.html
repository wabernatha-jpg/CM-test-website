You Don't Have to Want to Network. You Just Need a Framework.

I talked myself out of a first time networking event for a solid ten minutes before running a SWOT on myself instead. Here's what happened when the room didn't match a single thing I'd planned for.

Wesley Abernatha · September 10, 2026

The invite for the Friday morning networking event had been sitting on my calendar for two weeks. The morning of, I was doing what everyone does: telling myself I had too much else going on. Too many deliverables. Too many things I “really needed” to work on instead. It was a good story and I almost believed some of it. I knew I was making excuses, because the actual reason was I didn’t want to walk into a room full of strangers.

I’d already told myself I was doing one networking event a week, no exceptions, so backing out wasn’t really on the table. What got me out the door wasn’t willpower. It was the tools I already had to lean on, and my fiancée, who wasn’t hearing those excuses again and kindly let me know “Practice what you preach and set a goal.”

I asked myself what was in my control, what I was good at, and what I needed to stop doing. Then I looked at what wasn’t in my control and figured out how to leverage it anyway. The whole thing took less than a few minutes.

Turns out there’s a reason why “I should really go network more” quietly dies on a calendar, and why a specific plan doesn’t.

Why “Just Go” Doesn’t Work, and What Does

Psychologist Peter Gollwitzer has spent decades studying why some intentions turn into action and others quietly die in your calendar. His answer is almost embarrassingly simple: vague intentions (“I should network more”) rely on in-the-moment willpower, and willpower is exactly what’s missing when you’re an hour out from something you’re already dreading. Specific, structured plans (what he calls implementation intentions, built as “if X, then Y”) don’t rely on willpower at all. They pre-load the decision, so there’s nothing left to negotiate about when the moment arrives.1

The effect size is larger than you’d expect from something this simple. In one of Gollwitzer’s studies, students who wrote a specific if-then plan for when and where they’d do a piece of homework started, on average, about ninety minutes after their intended time. Students who only set a goal without the structured plan started closer to eight hours late.1 Other studies in his research program found the effect holding up even after people had already depleted their willpower on an unrelated task, and even in six-year-olds, which tells you this isn’t a discipline trick for disciplined people. It’s a structural fix for an unstructured problem.

The dread isn’t a character flaw. It’s what an unstructured task feels like from the inside. Structure is the fix, not motivation.

This is the piece most networking advice skips. “Just put yourself out there” is a goal, not a plan, and goals without plans are exactly what Gollwitzer’s research says will get quietly abandoned. What you actually need is a way to turn “networking” (vague, global, faintly humiliating) into a small number of specific, bounded decisions you’ve already made in advance.

Does Networking Even Matter That Much?

You’ve probably heard some version of “85% of jobs are filled through networking.” It’s one of the most repeated stats in career advice, and it traces back to a single non-random 2015-2016 survey of about 3,000 self-selected respondents whose actual results showed 42-62%, not 85%. It’s a bad stat, and repeating it doesn’t do the underlying argument any favors.2

The real evidence is older and better. In 1973, Stanford sociologist Mark Granovetter found people are more likely to land a job through weak ties (acquaintances, not close friends or family) because your close contacts mostly know what you already know.3 A 2022 field experiment across 20 million LinkedIn users confirmed it, with a twist: the relationship isn’t a straight line, it’s an inverted U. Total strangers underperform too. The sweet spot is moderately weak ties: someone you’ve met a couple of times, with maybe ten mutual connections.4

Employers see the same pattern from the other side: referred candidates get hired at roughly 30%, against about 7% through other channels, and land faster with better retention once they’re in the role.5 Two data sources, from opposite ends of hiring, land on the same conclusion, and it brings us back to that Friday I almost talked myself out of. The stakes are real. So is the dread. You need a way to hold both at once.

An Unlikely Assist From a 1960s Consulting Framework

Historical Tidbit
The Accidental Rename

That’s where SWOT comes in, and its own backstory is a useful reminder that frameworks are working tools, not sacred texts. SWOT was built between 1960 and 1970 by a Stanford Research Institute team (Robert Stewart, Albert Humphrey, and others) trying to figure out why corporate planning kept failing at Fortune 500 companies. It wasn’t even called SWOT at first: the original version was SOFT (Satisfactory, Opportunity, Fault, Threat), presented at a 1964 Zurich seminar. Two attendees swapped the F for a W on the spot. Humphrey’s own account: “This was later changed to SWOT – don’t ask.”6 The familiar two-by-two matrix wasn’t formalized until Heinz Weihrich did it in 1982. Even the credit is disputed: Humphrey later had to push back on Harvard and MIT staking a claim to it.6

The point isn’t trivia. It’s that a framework used by nearly every business school and strategy consultant on earth started as a scrappy, informally-named fix for a real problem. These frameworks aren’t limited to organizations, either. We need to practice them regularly, and figuring out how to walk into a room full of strangers is as good a place as any.

The Personal Networking SWOT

Applied to yourself instead of a company, SWOT does the same job it always does: it takes something too big to think about clearly and breaks it into four bounded questions.

Strengths
Years of experience, education and passion
Boots-on-the-ground work ethic
A toolbox of frameworks to pull from
A great support network
A steady book of nonprofit business
Weaknesses
Downplay what I do
A story for everything, and I want to tell all of them
Zero brand recognition in this room
Ramble when nervous
Opportunities
Multiple industries will be in the room
A real chance to promote what I do
New relationships
Learning about other events and organizations
Threats
No idea what to expect walking in
New guy, no track record with this group
Getting booed and laughed at! (Rated as pretty low likelihood)

Strengths took a few seconds to write down. Years of experience, a work ethic built from actually doing the work, and I know what I’m good at. I’ve got plenty of education, a stack of frameworks and resources ready to use, a strong support network, and a steady book of nonprofit business I almost forgot to write down.

Weaknesses (or “opportunities to improve,” if you want to be a bit easier on yourself) are next. I downplay my own experience, I have a story for everything and want to tell every single one of them, nobody in that room had heard of me yet, and I ramble when I’m nervous. Being awkward is my superpower sometimes.

Opportunities were a five-minute Google search and a scroll through the organization’s page and its sponsors, plus a little light LinkedIn stalking on a few names (I’ll admit it). There would be multiple industries in the room, a real shot at promoting what I do, new relationships, and a chance to learn about events I didn’t know existed.

Threats were mostly “I don’t know what to expect” and “I’m the new guy with zero track record here.” I also wrote down “everyone in the room boos me off the stage” and rated it very low likelihood, because writing it down is funnier than not. For the record, nobody booed.

The Room Didn’t Match the Plan

Here’s the part that actually mattered. I’d built an elevator pitch (scalable from thirty seconds to two minutes) aimed at a room of business owners and entrepreneurs. My SWOT hadn’t predicted the room I actually walked into: heavy with nonprofits and community caregivers instead. I didn’t have a nonprofit-specific pitch loaded and ready, but I had a different strength to lean on. I love working with nonprofits, so this is where I naturally fit in.

I did notice toward the end of my introduction I’d stopped looking anyone in the eye. That’s my signal that I’ve stopped talking to someone and started talking to hear myself talk. Because I’d written that weakness down an hour earlier, I caught it live instead of on the drive home, and wrapped the pitch up clean.

I walked out with real connections, not just cards in a pocket, and with next steps on all four boxes: one thing to actively fix, one strength I now know to lead with in a room like that, and a list of opportunities I hadn’t known existed until that Friday.

From Four Boxes to an If-Then Plan

A SWOT on its own is still just an analysis. The move that actually gets you through the door is converting each quadrant into the kind of if-then plan Gollwitzer’s research says works: pre-decide the response so there’s nothing left to negotiate on the night.

  • If the room doesn’t match what I prepared for, then I lean on my next-best strength instead of forcing the pitch I rehearsed.
  • If I need to adjust on the fly, then I’ve already scaled the pitch from thirty seconds to two minutes, ready either way.
  • If I notice I’ve stopped making eye contact, then I know I’m rambling, and I wrap it up.
  • If it’s been a week since I met someone worth staying in touch with, then I send the follow-up that day, not “eventually.”

None of these require you to become a different person. They require you to make four decisions once, in advance, calmly, instead of remaking them from scratch, under stress, an hour before you almost don’t go.

The Actual Lesson Isn’t About Networking

Widen the lens and the pattern generalizes past this one dreaded event. Difficult conversations, salary negotiations, career pivots, asking for help: almost everything people habitually avoid shares the same shape. It’s important, it’s ambiguous, and the ambiguity itself is what triggers the avoidance. The fix in every case is structurally identical to what I did with a SWOT and a Friday night mixer. Take the vague, threatening version of the task, break it into a small number of bounded questions, and convert the answers into if-then plans you’ve already made. The framework changes. The mechanism doesn’t.

None of this works if a SWOT is a tool you pick up for the first time when the stakes are already high. I reach for frameworks out of habit because I practice on things that don’t matter yet. I’ve written elsewhere about doing this on purpose, on stuff as low-stakes as how you make coffee, specifically so the muscle is there when a room full of strangers is on the line.

That’s the actual argument here, and networking is just the clearest place to see it work: frameworks aren’t corporate décor for strategy decks. They’re one of the most reliable tools available for getting yourself to do the specific, high-value thing you keep avoiding.

The invite is still sitting in your calendar. Go anyway. You don’t need to want to. You just need a plan for what happens when you walk in.
