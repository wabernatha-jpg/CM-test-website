SMART Goals Are a Baseline, Not a Formula

The five letters work. They work even better once you've practiced bending them somewhere the stakes are low.

Wesley Abernatha · July 17, 2026

Most people can recite SMART on command. Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound: it shows up in performance reviews, project kickoffs, and every goal-setting slide deck built in the last forty years. Ask the same person to write one down for something that actually matters to them, though, and the fluency disappears. They know the letters. They have never practiced applying them to anything that wasn’t handed to them by a manager.

That gap matters more than it looks like it should. A framework you have only ever used in a formal, high-stakes setting is a framework you are still learning the mechanics of at the exact moment you need to be thinking about the problem instead. Pilots rehearse emergency checklists in a simulator long before a real one matters. Athletes drill a free throw thousands of times in an empty gym so the form is automatic when a game is on the line. Goal-setting works the same way. The letters should already be second nature by the time a real stretch goal, a program deadline, or a grant requirement puts weight on them.

What SMART Actually Means

The baseline is simple enough to walk through once and move past.

01
Specific
Name the exact outcome, not a direction. "Get better at X" isn't a goal yet, it's a topic.
02
Measurable
Attach a number or a checkable event, something you can point to and say yes or no, done or not.
03
Achievable
A real stretch, calibrated to where you're honestly starting, not where you wish you were starting.
04
Relevant
Tied to something that matters right now, not a someday goal borrowed from someone else's priorities.
05
Time-bound
A real date on a real calendar. "Eventually" isn't a deadline, it's permission to never start.

Historical Tidbit
The Consultant Who Named the Checklist

The acronym has a small, specific origin: in November 1981, a corporate planning director named George T. Doran published it as a checklist for writing better internal objectives at one company, in one trade-magazine article.1 Four decades later it’s the default vocabulary for goal-setting everywhere from youth sports to nonprofit boardrooms, built for spreadsheets, not for a volunteer-run program trying to keep its shelves straight.

90%+
Of Studies Reviewed Found Specific, Hard Goals Beat Vague Ones

Here’s the case for going through the trouble of naming a real number and a real date instead of a vague direction: in a review of roughly three decades of goal-setting research, Edwin Locke and Gary Latham found that specific, challenging goals outperformed vague “do your best” goals in the large majority of studies they examined.2 The letters aren’t corporate ritual. They’re the mechanism that makes a goal work at all, which is exactly why it matters which one you lean on.

The Baseline Is a Starting Point, Not a Cage

Doran’s original checklist assumed a manager writing a business objective, so all five letters lean toward hard numbers on a fixed schedule. That fits a grant deliverable or a finance cleanup cleanly. It fits a first attempt at a new physical skill, or a scrappy program with no real system behind it yet, much less cleanly, and forcing the same weighting onto every situation is where SMART goals earn their reputation as corporate busywork bolted onto work that was never corporate to begin with. The fix isn’t to abandon the structure. It’s to recognize which letter needs to flex for the situation in front of you, something that only feels obvious once you’ve practiced doing it somewhere the outcome doesn’t matter much.

Three Goals, Three Different Stretches

Personal: Learning to Juggle a Soccer Ball From Zero

Three months ago, she couldn’t get a soccer ball off the ground twice in a row. She didn’t set out to master freestyle tricks. She picked one number to beat: touches on her weaker foot before the ball hit the ground, checked every night for two weeks. Day one, that number was one. By the end of week two, it was five, so she reset the target and kept going. The goal was never “get good at juggling.” It was a number small enough to actually hit, and honest enough about where she was starting that missing it wasn’t an option.

Here the letter doing the most work was Achievable. A beginner who sets the bar at where they want to be, instead of where they are, quits by day three and blames the framework instead of the target.

Program Operations: A Food Pantry’s Weekly Inventory

A local food pantry I work with had a problem that doesn’t show up until the shelves are already wrong: some items ran short while other donations sat until they expired, because nobody had a current, reliable count of what was actually on the shelf. “Get inventory under control” wasn’t a goal, it was a complaint. The real goal was specific: a complete inventory count, every week, finished in under two hours, with everything moved onto a strict first-in-first-out rotation so nothing sat forgotten behind a newer donation again.

Here Measurable was the letter doing the most work. “Better inventory” isn’t checkable. A full count, on a set cadence, inside a time limit tight enough that volunteers would actually keep doing it, is.

Program Delivery: A Volunteer Retention Target

The program team had missed its volunteer-retention goal twice before, both times with a target that amounted to “get more people to stick around.” The third attempt named the number: cut first-shift no-shows by a defined percentage, measured against last quarter’s actual numbers instead of a hopeful guess, by the end of the quarter. Nothing about the format was clever. It was the same five letters everyone already knew, actually followed this time instead of gestured at, and it was the first version that hit.

Nothing here needed bending. That’s the point: the textbook case is the exception, not the pattern, which is exactly why so many people assume the plain formula always applies.

Practice It Before You Need It

The point of working through a beginner’s juggling goal or a food pantry’s inventory fix isn’t that either one is as consequential as a quarterly program target. It’s that both are low-stakes enough to fail at without real cost, which makes them the right place to get comfortable with the actual skill underneath SMART goals: deciding which letter needs to flex, and by how much. Wait until a stretch project or a board-level deadline to figure that out for the first time, and half the effort goes into relearning the framework instead of solving the problem. Practice it somewhere small and personal first, and by the time it counts for your organization, the only thing left to think about is the goal itself.

Key Takeaways

Key Frameworks:

  • SMART goals (Doran, 1981): the five-part baseline structure of Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound, and which letter typically needs to flex depending on whether the goal is a new skill, an operational fix, or a numbers-driven program target.

Try It: This week, write one full SMART goal for something entirely outside your day job, a hobby, a habit, or a program at an organization you volunteer with. Give it a real number or checkable behavior and a real date, not a vague direction.
