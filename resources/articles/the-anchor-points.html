Resilience

Your Situation Can Fall Apart. You Don't Have To.

How a false accusation nearly cost me my kids, my house, and my career, and the four anchors that kept me from becoming someone I didn't recognize while I fought to keep all three.

Wesley Abernatha · July 27, 2026

I was about three months into a new job when I was accused of something horrendous. Something I hadn’t done. Something that, by the time it was finally squashed, there was no question I hadn’t done. But squashed took a long time to arrive, and for most of that stretch I was living with the very real possibility of losing my kids, my house, my car, my entire life savings, even the money set aside for my kids’ college. I genuinely thought I might end up in prison for something that never happened.

And I still had to show up to work every day and perform.

At first I handled it by disappearing into it quietly. I stopped being early. Then I stopped being on time. My lawyer’s calls and texts came in constantly, at the worst possible moments, and I’d drift out of a meeting mid-sentence trying to figure out how to just survive the week. I told almost no one. I thought silence was the responsible move, the professional one. Keep it contained, keep it separate, keep performing.

It didn’t stay contained. It came out sideways instead.

I work in continuous improvement and change management, which means my job runs almost entirely on collaboration. Building on someone else’s idea out loud, in the room, in real time, is the whole method. And somewhere in the middle of the worst months of my life, I started treating that same collaborative back-and-forth as a threat. I argued with people I had no real disagreement with. I picked fights with a senior colleague over things that, looking back, weren’t worth a raised voice, let alone a standoff. I went out of my way to be argumentative, is the honest way to put it, and I didn’t fully see it happening while it was happening. I just knew that a group I used to enjoy being in had started to feel like a group I had to defend myself in. I’d always been the fun-loving guy people wanted around. For a while, I became the hard-ass grump nobody wanted to bring a half-formed idea to.

The accusation was the crisis. But the thing that could have actually cost me my job wasn’t the accusation. It was where all of that fear and exhaustion went once it had nowhere else to go: sideways, onto people who had absolutely nothing to do with any of it.

The Four Things That Actually Held

I didn’t sit down and design a framework. I found it the way you find most things that save you during a genuinely bad stretch: by accident, under pressure, one week at a time, and only in hindsight did I notice the same four moves kept showing up whenever a week went better than the one before it.

Four moves. None of them make the crisis smaller. What they do is keep the crisis from being the only thing steering you.

Anchor One: Find Your Allies

For a long time I treated silence as strength. Say nothing, absorb it alone, keep the two halves of my life sealed off from each other. What that actually did was leave me managing an active legal nightmare with zero support inside the eight to ten hours a day I spent at work, which is most of the hours I had.

Eventually I told my manager. Not the whole story, not close to it, just enough for him to understand why I wasn’t fully myself and why my hours might need to flex. He didn’t flinch. It turned out he’d been carrying his own version of chaos, trying to be a present dad through something hard of his own, and he’d just never said so either. One honest conversation and I had a person at work who could correctly read my worst days as “something’s going on,” instead of filing them away as attitude or a performance problem.

This isn’t just a nice feeling. Social support has a specific, well-documented function during stress: it buffers you. Decades of research on the subject draw a real distinction between support that just generally makes life nicer and support that actively protects you from the harm a stressful event would otherwise do, and it’s the second kind that matters most when things are genuinely bad.1 You don’t need to hand someone your whole story to get it. You need one or two people who know enough to catch you, not carry you.

Anchor Two: Sort What You Can and Can’t Control

Here’s the pivot point, and it’s the one I’d point to first if someone asked me where the actual turnaround happened.

I couldn’t control the accusation, the legal timeline, my lawyer’s phone calls, or the outcome. What I could control, it turned out, was almost everything happening inside the walls of my office. I set firmer working hours instead of letting the day bleed formlessly into the crisis and back. I got honest about which commitments I could hold and which ones I needed to renegotiate. I started asking, deliberately, before every reaction: is this actually about the thing in front of me, or is this about the thing I can’t do anything about right now?

That question is doing more work than it looks like. People who orient toward what their own actions can actually change, rather than fixating on forces entirely outside their hands, consistently cope with stress better than people stuck in the second mode.2 It’s not positive thinking. It’s triage. Sort the pile into “mine to move” and “not mine to move,” and stop spending energy on the second pile, because you don’t have any to spare.

Anchor Three: Let Effort Be Your Shield

Here’s the part that surprised me most: I had to crush it at work. Not survive it, crush it. I let myself work extra hard on the days I needed an outlet, and some of my best output came out of the worst weeks of my life.

But I had to watch that closely, because the same behavior can mean two completely different things. Burying yourself in work can be genuine engagement, effort aimed at something real and solvable. Or it can be avoidance wearing a productive-looking costume, distraction with nothing actually behind it. Research on coping draws exactly this line: task-focused effort, working directly on a real, solvable problem, holds up over time, while avoidance-based coping, distraction with no connection to what’s actually wrong, tends to fall apart the longer a crisis goes on.3 Same activity, opposite outcomes, depending on which one you’re actually doing.

So the discipline was never “work more.” It was checking in with myself on which one was happening. Effort as armor, chosen on purpose, holds up. Effort as a hiding place doesn’t, and eventually it stops protecting you at all.

Anchor Four: Give Yourself a Win

The last piece is the one that kept me from quitting on myself entirely: I stopped waiting for big wins and started manufacturing small ones on purpose.

I cut projects into the smallest pieces I could reasonably finish, and I finished one, often, even on the worst days. Not because the small piece mattered that much on its own, but because I needed proof, regularly, that I was still capable, still moving, still getting somewhere, even while everything else in my life said otherwise. Just because life is falling apart doesn’t mean you get to coast. If anything it’s the opposite: you have to work harder during the worst stretches, specifically so that when you’re tempted to say the hell with all of it and quit everything, you can look back at the last few weeks and see real, undeniable proof that you kept going. That you’re still a little bit of a superhero, even on the days it doesn’t feel like it.

Progress Predicted the Good Days.

This isn’t just a feel-good trick. A multi-year study tracking nearly 12,000 daily diary entries from employees across seven companies found that the single strongest predictor of a good day at work, in mood, motivation, and how people felt about their job, was visible forward progress on something that mattered, no matter how small.4 Concrete wins compound, too. They’re harder to undo than big, ambitious plans, they require less coordination to pull off, and they hold up even when everything around them is in chaos.5 Give yourself one today. Then another tomorrow.

Why the Anchors Hold Even When Nothing Else Does

None of this fixed the actual situation. My case had its own timeline, and no framework speeds up a legal process or gives you your savings back early. What the four anchors did was something narrower and, it turned out, more important: they kept me from losing myself while I waited it out.

I’m not the hard-ass grump I became for a while. I found my way back to being the guy people actually want in the room, the one who builds on an idea instead of attacking it. But I didn’t get there by pretending the worst months of my life didn’t happen, and I didn’t get there by becoming someone harder and colder to survive them. I got there by remembering that my situation and my feelings were always going to go up and down, wildly, on their own schedule, completely out of my control. My values and who I actually am were never supposed to move with them. That’s the whole point of an anchor. It doesn’t stop the water from getting rough. It just makes sure you’re still there, exactly where you’re supposed to be, when it finally calms back down.

Key Takeaways

Key Frameworks:

  • The Anchor Points: find your allies, sort what you can and can’t control, let effort be your shield, and give yourself a win, four deliberate moves that hold you steady without requiring you to become someone else.

Try It: Before your next 1:1 or check-in, write one honest sentence naming what’s actually going on for you right now, even if you never say it out loud to anyone. Then draw two columns: everything today that you can act on, and everything you can’t. Cross out the second column entirely, and before you go home, finish one thing, however small, from the first.
